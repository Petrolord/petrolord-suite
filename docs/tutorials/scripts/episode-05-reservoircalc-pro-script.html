Before you record

  1. Publish the surface and the zone first. Section 04 pulls porosity, water saturation and net to gross from a registry zone, and the area from a registry surface. Both have to exist before the take. Use the zone you built in Episode 2 and the surface you gridded in Episode 4, so the series visibly compounds.
  2. Choose a structure where moving the contact matters. Section 06 moves the oil water contact by fifty feet on camera and the volume has to visibly change. On a very steep structure it will barely move and the beat falls flat. Rehearse it and pick a shift that reads well.
  3. Have the boundary polygon to hand. The area of interest beat in section 06 uses the boundary you drew in Mapping and Surface Studio. Import it through the Wells tab earlier in the take so it is already in the list.
  4. Render section by section, honour the pauses. Same as every episode. The two pause marks are a contact recalculating and a Monte Carlo run, and both take real time.
The argument this episode is making. A volume is a range with a geometry behind it. Four beats carry that: the gross versus net warning in section 03, the contact demonstration in section 06, the tornado read as an instruction in section 09, and the audit trail in section 10. If anything gets cut, do not cut those.
Break point. Marked after section 06, where the deterministic structural case is complete. Part 1 runs 13:35 and Part 2 runs about 9:50, and the split is cleaner than most because Part 2 is entirely about uncertainty.

There is one mistake worth naming out loud here, because it is the most common one in volumetrics anywhere. The thickness field is your gross interval. Net rock is worked out for you, as gross times net to gross. So if you type your net pay into the thickness box and also leave net to gross below one, you have applied the net cut twice, and your volume is too small by exactly that factor. Gross goes in the thickness box. Always.

Now the contacts. On the Geo tab, the fluid contacts card takes your oil water contact, and for an oil and gas case the gas oil contact as well. These are elevations, true vertical depth subsea, and the convention is the same one you have seen all series. Negative below datum. Minus eight thousand is deeper than minus seven thousand.

Point at the unit selector beside the contact field.

The unit selector beside them defaults to the depth unit your account already uses across Geoscience, so the number you type here is the same number you read off the map.

Hold on the recalculated result, then on the contoured surface.

And with that, the calculation changes completely. Instead of multiplying an area by a thickness, the app lays a grid over your top surface and works out, cell by cell, how much of the reservoir column at that cell falls inside the fluid window. Then it adds them all up.

Switch input method back to simple for three seconds, change the contact, show nothing happening, then return to hybrid.

One rule to remember here. Contacts only bite in hybrid and surfaces. The simple method has no depth reference at all, so it cannot know where a contact is, and it ignores them. If you type a contact and nothing happens, that is why.

The volume moved, and it did not move by fifty feet times your area. It moved by however much rock actually sits between the old contact and the new one on that particular structure. On a steep flank that is a small number. On a broad flat crest it is enormous.

Which is exactly the point. A domed top and a flat top at the same mean depth give different volumes, because they are different shapes. Area times thickness cannot tell you that. Integrating on the real surface can, and that is what your mapping work in the last episode bought you.

Set it active, and the integration is clipped to it. Everything outside stops counting. That is how you get the volume of one fault block, or one licence area, or the part of the structure you actually hold, rather than the whole map.

07

Uncertainty: choosing the distributions

13:35 · 2:32 · 380 w
Screen
Voice over

Right panel showing the single deterministic figure. Hold on it.

You now have a number. And a single number is not an answer, because not one of the inputs behind it is known exactly.

Project Settings, change Mode to probabilistic. The left panel changes to the simulation setup and the three step panel.

So change Mode to probabilistic. The left panel is replaced by a simulation setup card and a three step panel. Get your deterministic case right before you do this, because the study centres its distributions on that base case.

Step 1, Distributions. Open the distribution type selector on porosity so all five options are readable.

Step one is the distributions. For each variable you choose a shape and its parameters. Triangular, when you have a low, a most likely and a high, which is how most subsurface uncertainty actually gets quoted. Normal, when you have a mean and a standard deviation. Lognormal, for quantities that cannot go below zero and have a long tail upward, which is most volumes and most permeabilities. Uniform, when you genuinely know nothing between two bounds. Or constant, when you are certain and want it held fixed.

Set a triangular distribution on porosity, typing a low, a mode and a high.

Be honest here rather than conservative. A distribution that is too narrow does not make your project safer. It makes your range wrong, and it hides the fact that you do not know something.

Step 2, Settings. Point at the correlated sampling and the automatic porosity to water saturation correlation.

Step two holds the settings, and two of them deserve your attention. The sampler applies a negative correlation between porosity and water saturation by default, because in real rock those two move against each other. Sampling them independently would give you a tail of cases with high porosity and high water saturation together, and that rock does not exist.

Point at Base-Case Consistency Mode, on by default.

Base case consistency mode, which is on by default, checks that your distributions really are centred on the deterministic case you just built. It is advisory. It will tell you when they have drifted apart, and it will not stop you.

The right panel now holds a range instead of a number. P ninety, P fifty, P ten, the mean, and the spread between them.

Zoom the P90 card labelled Proven, then the P10 card labelled Possible.

And the labels beside them are not decoration. P ninety is the volume you have a ninety percent chance of meeting or exceeding, which is why it is labelled proven. P ten is the optimistic end. P fifty sits in the middle, and it is the number to quote when somebody wants one number, because half your realisations came in above it and half below.

Frame the mean and the P50 together so any difference between them is visible.

Look at the mean sitting beside the P fifty. On a skewed distribution, and volumes are almost always skewed, those two are not the same number. If they are far apart, your case has a long tail, and that is worth understanding before you average it away.

Click View Full Analysis. Hold on the histogram, then scroll to the cumulative curve and trace a percentile with the cursor.

View Full Analysis opens the histogram and the cumulative curve. The histogram shows you the shape of the answer. The cumulative curve is where you read off any percentile you like, not only the three on the cards.

And then the chart that should change what you do next. The tornado.

Point at the longest bar at the top, then run the cursor down to the shortest.

Each bar is one input, and its length is how much of the variance in your answer that input is responsible for. They are sorted, so the one at the top is what your range is actually made of.

Hold on the top bar alone.

Read that top bar as an instruction. It is telling you where the next pound of your appraisal budget should go. If the tornado says your range is driven by net to gross, then another well that improves your net to gross estimate is worth more than a better P. V. T. sample, however much you would enjoy the P. V. T. sample.

Point at the two or three shortest bars at the bottom.

And the bars at the bottom are just as useful. Those are the inputs somebody argued about in a meeting that turn out not to matter. Stop arguing about them.
