Before you record

  1. A well with a sonic curve, and ideally two wells. The sonic is required. Section 02 contrasts a well that has a density curve with one that does not, where the app substitutes Gardner densities and says so. Having both to hand makes that beat concrete instead of theoretical.
  2. Know which interval is normally pressured and which is overpressured. Section 05 deliberately puts a compaction trend pick in the wrong place to show the prognosis collapse. That is the most important teaching moment in the episode, and it only works if you can produce it on demand.
  3. Bring real calibration data if you can. Formation tests, leak-off tests, or mud weights that worked and mud weights that did not. Section 07 is far stronger with real dots on the chart than with invented ones.
  4. Have a velocity model from Seismolord. Section 08 runs a prognosis with no well at all from a velocity trend calibrated in Episode 6. Without one that section has nothing to show.
  5. Render section by section, honour the pause. The single pause is the compaction trend fitting and writing back into the dock.
The argument this episode is making. Pore pressure is not measured. It is inferred from a trend you fit, through a method you choose, with an exponent you type. Section 01 states it. Section 05 shows the prognosis collapsing when a trend pick lands in overpressured rock. Section 06 warns against tuning the exponent until the answer looks right. Section 07 explains why the calibration points deliberately change nothing. Section 10 closes it. Those five carry the episode.
This is the drilling handoff. Section 09 says the published curves are a contract the mechanical earth model reads. If the series continues into the Drilling module, this episode is the hinge, so give that beat its full weight.

Where geoscience becomes a drilling decision

Dashboard, Geoscience module. Hold on the Pore Pressure Studio tile, then open it.

Every application in this series so far has produced something a geoscientist reads. This one produces a number somebody drills on.

The workspace with a full prognosis showing. Zoom the ribbon readout with the pressure unit set to pounds per gallon.

Pore Pressure Studio takes a well's sonic and density curves and turns them into a pressure prognosis. The weight of everything above you. The pressure of the water in the pores. And the pressure at which the rock breaks. It will hand you all three as an equivalent mud weight, which is the form a well plan actually uses.

Quick cuts, two seconds each: the compaction trend chart, the method parameters in the dock, the published curves listed in Well Data Manager.

It is worth being honest at the start about what this is. Pore pressure is not measured. It is inferred, from a trend you fit yourself, through a method you choose, with an exponent you type. Every one of those is a decision, and this application puts all of them in front of you rather than behind a default.

Hold on the full workspace, then move toward the well list.

In this video we will load a well, set the water column, fit a compaction trend, run both methods, and publish a prognosis the drilling side can read. Let us pick a well.

03

Units, and the datum that catches people

03:38 · 2:24 · 360 w
Screen
Voice over

Ribbon, Units. Open the pressure selector showing all four choices.

Before anything else, the units. Pressure can be read as megapascals, as pounds per square inch, or as an equivalent mud weight, in pounds per gallon or in specific gravity.

Switch from megapascals to pounds per gallon. Hold wide so the readout, both charts and the dock fields all convert together.

Change it and everything moves together. The readout, both charts, the fields in the dock and your calibration points. Even the density fields follow, because the density you want beside a pressure in pounds per square inch is not the one you want beside megapascals.

Hover the tooltip stating that everything stored and computed is SI.

And underneath, nothing changes. The engine, the saved project and the published curves are all in pascals and metres, always. The unit selectors are a lens, not a conversion of your work.

Hold on an equivalent mud weight value in the readout.

Now the part that catches people. An equivalent mud weight is not a pressure. It is a pressure divided by a depth, so it depends entirely on which depth you divide by.

Set the mudline field to zero and Apply. Read a shallow depth. Then set it correctly, Apply, and read the same depth again.

With the mudline field set, the datum is the rotary table, and the depth is the mudline measured depth plus the depth below it. Leave that field at zero and the datum becomes sea level instead, so every shallow sample carries the whole water column in its depth, and your equivalent mud weight near the top looks far too low.

Hover the readout's unit tooltip, then point at the chart axis label naming the datum.

The app never leaves you guessing which one is in use. The readout's tooltip says it, the chart axis says it, and it is written into the header of the file you export. If a mud weight ever looks wrong, this is the first thing to check, and it is usually the answer.

04

The water column, and two of the four curves

06:02 · 2:00 · 300 w
Screen
Voice over

Dock, Water column group. Type the water depth, the seawater density and the pore fluid density.

The Water column group at the top of the dock sets the environment. The water depth, the density of the seawater above the mudline, and the density of the fluid in the pores.

Click Apply. The chart redraws.

Type them and press Apply. Everything in this dock is a draft until you do.

Prognosis chart. Trace the overburden curve with the cursor from top to bottom.

Those three numbers build two of the four curves on your chart. The overburden integrates the seawater column over the water depth, and then the bulk density of the rock all the way down the well. It is the weight of everything above.

Trace the hydrostatic curve.

And the hydrostatic is your pore fluid density, times gravity, times the depth below the sea surface. That is what the pressure would be if the pore fluid were simply connected to the surface, which in a normally pressured section it effectively is.

Point at where the pore pressure curve sits on the hydrostatic, then at where it climbs away from it.

Which makes the hydrostatic the line that matters most on this chart. Wherever your pore pressure sits on it, the section is normally pressured. Wherever it climbs away from it, you have overpressure, and how far it climbs is what the rest of this application exists to estimate.

05

The normal compaction trend

08:02 · 2:51 · 420 w
Screen
Voice over

Click the NCT view. The measured sonic and the trend draw.

So, the estimate. Switch to the normal compaction trend view.

Hold on both curves. Trace the trend, then the measured transit time.

Two curves. Your measured transit time against depth, and the trend a normally pressured shale would follow. That trend is the matrix transit time plus a mudline excess that decays exponentially as the rock compacts.

Point at a depth where the two agree, then at a depth where the measured curve is slower.

Where those two agree, the rock has compacted normally. Where the measured curve is slower than the trend, the rock is undercompacted. It is holding fluid that should have been squeezed out, and that fluid is carrying part of the load. That gap is the entire basis of the pore pressure estimate.

Type a depth in a normally pressured shale, click Add pick. Repeat three more times at different depths.

Which means the trend had better be right. Add picks at depths you believe are normally pressured, in shale. You type the depth and the app takes the transit time from the nearest sample, so you are picking a depth rather than eyeballing a value.

Click Fit NCT.

Then press Fit.

Pause 3 seconds. The trend redraws and the values write into the dock. Cut to the dock to show them.

Show the dock's mudline transit time and compaction constant, now populated. Point at the matrix value, unchanged.

It solves the mudline transit time and the compaction constant exactly from your picks and writes them straight into the dock. The matrix transit time is left alone, because that is a property of the rock and not something your picks can tell you. And it needs at least two picks, at different depths, with transit times above the matrix value. Fewer than that and it refuses rather than fitting something meaningless.

Add a pick inside the overpressured section, refit, and hold on the trend moving and the pore pressure curve collapsing toward hydrostatic. Then remove it and refit.

And here is the warning worth taking seriously. Put a pick in a section that is already overpressured, and you have fitted your normal trend to abnormal rock. The trend moves toward the data, the gap closes, and your prognosis quietly says everything is fine. This is the single biggest lever in the application, and it is a curve you drew.

06

Eaton and Bowers

10:53 · 2:52 · 430 w
Screen
Voice over

Dock, Method group. Show both buttons.

With a trend in place, choose a method. There are two, and they think about the problem differently.

Select Eaton. The exponent field appears.

Eaton is the classic. It takes the effective stress the overburden would produce, and scales it by the ratio of the normal transit time to the measured one, raised to an exponent.

Set the exponent to three and Apply. Then try one point five, then five, holding on the prognosis moving each time. Return to three.

That exponent is the whole method in one number. Three is the published value for sonic, and it is where you should start. Change it and watch the prognosis move. If you find yourself tuning the exponent until the answer matches what you expected, stop, because you are no longer using a method. You are drawing a curve.

Select Bowers. The four fields appear. Point at the A and B fields.

Bowers works from the other direction. It inverts the velocity to an effective stress through a loading curve, with two coefficients, A and B, published in feet per second and pounds per square inch, which is why those fields ask for those units regardless of what you are displaying.

Leave the U field empty and Apply. Then fill U and the maximum effective stress and Apply again.

Leave the U field empty and you get the loading curve alone, which suits a section where the pressure came from rapid burial. Fill in U and the maximum effective stress, and the unloading curve applies wherever the velocity has reversed.

Cut to the NCT view showing a velocity reversal, then back to the prognosis at that depth.

That matters, because a velocity reversal means something specific. The rock was buried, it compacted, and then the pressure came up afterwards, from fluid expansion or from being charged from below. Rock that has been unloaded does not give its velocity back, so a loading curve alone will underestimate the pressure there. Bowers with an unloading branch is how you handle it.

Switch between Eaton and Bowers twice, holding on the difference in the prognosis.

Run both. If they agree, that is worth something. If they disagree badly, the disagreement is telling you which mechanism your section actually has, and that is more useful than either answer on its own.

07

The drilling window, and the calibration points

13:45 · 2:00 · 300 w
Screen
Voice over

Dock, the Poisson's ratio field. Then the prognosis chart, tracing the fracture curve.

The fourth curve is the fracture pressure, and it comes from one number. Poisson's ratio.

Shade or point along the gap between the pore pressure and fracture pressure curves.

It estimates the minimum horizontal stress as the pore pressure plus the effective overburden, scaled by the ratio of Poisson's ratio to one minus it. And the gap between the pore pressure and the fracture pressure is your drilling window. That gap is the reason this whole application exists, because it is what decides where casing has to go.

Dock, the calibration box. Type two or three lines of depth and pressure.

Then the calibration box. Type a depth and a pressure on each line, in whatever units you are displaying. Formation tests, leak-off tests, mud weights that worked and mud weights that did not.

Click Apply. The points appear as dots on the prognosis.

They appear as dots on the prognosis. And they change nothing. The app does not fit to them, does not bend a curve toward them, and does not warn you when it misses them.

Hold on a calibration dot that the prognosis misses.

That is deliberate, and it puts the judgment where it belongs. Your job is to look at those dots and decide whether the model is close enough. If it is not, go back and change the trend, or the exponent, or the method, for a reason you can state out loud. Not because a button moved the curve for you.

One other input is worth knowing about. Below the wells sits a list of seismic velocity trends. Those are the velocity models you calibrated in Seismolord, back in the sixth episode.

The prognosis draws from the trend. Zoom the trend-grade badge and hover its tooltip.

Choose one and you get a prognosis with no well at all. That is genuinely useful before you have drilled anything. But look at the badge. It says trend-grade, and it means exactly what it says. A regional velocity trend constrains the regional pressure trend. It carries no local anomaly, because a smooth function of depth cannot contain one.

Point at the Publish button, unavailable while a trend is selected.

And Publish is unavailable for a trend, because there is no well to write it to. Use it to frame the problem before you spud. Do not use it to plan a casing shoe.

Publish, export, save

Select the well again. Click Publish. Hold on the confirmation.

When the prognosis is one you will stand behind, publish it.

Cut to Well Data Manager, Logs tab, showing the three new curves with their provenance.

It writes three curves back to the well in the registry. Pore pressure, fracture pressure and overburden, in megapascals, with the method, the parameters and the input curves recorded in the provenance.

Change a parameter, Apply, publish again. Show the three curves replaced and other applications' curves untouched.

Publish again from the same project and those three are replaced. Curves that other applications published are left alone. So iterating on your prognosis does not quietly accumulate five versions of pore pressure on the same well.

Hover the Publish tooltip. Hold so the wording is readable.

And this is a contract, not just a convenience. Those three curves are what the mechanical earth model on the drilling side reads. When you publish, you are handing your interpretation to the people who will use it to choose a mud weight.

Click Prognosis CSV. Open the file and hold on the mud weight columns and the datum line in the header.

Prognosis C. S. V. downloads the whole profile in your chosen units, with equivalent mud weight columns in both pounds per gallon and specific gravity, and the datum written into the header. That is the table a well plan needs, in the form it needs it.

Click Save. Hold on the confirmation.

And Save keeps your parameters, your picks and your calibration points as a project, so the prognosis is reproducible rather than remembered.
