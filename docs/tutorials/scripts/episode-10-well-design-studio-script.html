Before you record

  1. Publish this one as two parts. Twenty five minutes is too long for one sitting, and the halves divide cleanly. Part 1 designs the well. Part 2 is about how wrong it might be and who else is down there. The break is marked after section 06.
  2. The Episode 9 payoff needs both halves in place. Section 10 hangs the pore pressure and fracture gradient curves on the trajectory. That needs a prognosis published from Pore Pressure Studio onto a registry well, and this plan published and bridged. Set the prognosis up before the take so the toggle works on camera.
  3. You need a neighbour. Section 08 scans for collisions and needs something to scan against: another wellbore on the pad, or a registry well in the same coordinate system. Without one the whole section is a shrug.
  4. Bring a real survey file. Section 09 imports one. An Excel workbook is the better demo than a CSV, because picking a sheet and previewing the parse shows more of what the importer does.
  5. Settle your site before you start. The coordinate system, the north reference and the wellhead coordinates all get typed in section 02 and used everywhere after. Improvising them on camera reads as guessing, and this is the one application in the series where guessing has consequences.
The argument this episode is making. A trajectory is a line. A well plan is a line plus how wrong it might be. Section 03 shows the north chain that decides where the line points. Section 06 makes the saved trajectory the authority rather than the editor. Section 07 puts an error model on every station. Section 08 shows the same covariances driving both the picture and the separation rule, so the two can never disagree. Those four carry it.
Pitch it to the right room. This is a directional driller's application and the terminology is dense. Do not soften it. Anyone watching Episode 10 of a subsurface series already knows what a dogleg is, and hedging the language will lose them faster than the jargon will.

Sites, wellbores and designs

01:40 · 2:20 · 350 w
Screen
Voice over

Tree header, click New site. The dialog opens.

Everything hangs off a site. A site is a pad, a platform or a location, and it carries the things every well drilled from it shares.

Type the site name, then open the CRS picker and choose the projected system.

Name it, then choose the projected coordinate reference system your coordinates are in. That field is not decoration. It places your wells on the earth, it drives the magnetic lookup we are about to use, and it is what lines your pad up with the seismic and the registry wells from earlier episodes.

Set the north reference and the origin. Add two pad slots and a lease line.

Set the north reference and the origin, and add your pad slots and lease lines if you have them. Those slots become the wellhead positions you can pick from later, and the lease lines draw on every plan view so you can see when a lateral is heading somewhere it should not.

Site menu, New wellbore. The dialog opens. Pick a slot, then set kelly bushing elevation, depth unit and azimuth reference.

Then a wellbore. Pick a slot, or type the wellhead coordinates directly. Set the kelly bushing elevation, the depth unit and the azimuth reference your team plans in.

Zoom the live magnetic values in the dialog: declination, dip, total field and grid convergence.

And watch what the dialog does while you type. It looks up the magnetic declination, the dip and the total field at that location, along with the grid convergence, and shows them to you live. Saving the wellbore caches all four onto it.

Wellbore menu, add a design. Show the tree: site, wellbore, design.

Under a wellbore sit designs. One wellbore can carry several, which is how you keep an original plan beside a sidetrack, or a base case beside an alternative, without either overwriting the other.

03

Grid, true and magnetic north

04:00 · 2:20 · 350 w
Screen
Voice over

Wellbore dialog, open the azimuth reference selector showing grid, true and magnetic.

That azimuth reference field deserves two minutes on its own, because getting it wrong points your well in the wrong direction and nothing on screen looks broken.

Choose grid. Hold.

There are three norths. Grid north is the map north of your coordinate system. It is what this application stores and computes in, always.

Choose true, then magnetic, holding on each.

True north is geographic north, and it differs from grid north by the convergence, which depends on where you are in the projection. Magnetic north is what the measurement while drilling tool actually senses, and it differs from true north by the declination, which changes with place and with time.

Survey listing on the Design tab, zoom the column header reading Azi grid.

Your wellbore's setting declares which of those you type and which you read back. And every field is labelled with it. The listings say Azi grid explicitly rather than leaving you to assume, and every conversion runs through the convergence and declination cached on the wellbore.

Set a non-grid reference on a wellbore with no cached magnetics. Hold on the warning.

And if you set a non-grid reference on a wellbore that has no cached magnetic model, it warns you loudly and treats your input as grid until you re-save the wellbore. It refuses to silently convert using numbers it does not have.

Point back at the declination value in the wellbore dialog.

The magnetic values come from the World Magnetic Model, computed inside the application from the official coefficients and checked against every one of the twenty four published test points. Re-save the wellbore to refresh them, and note that survey runs carry their own reference per run, because the tool that measured them did.

04

Targets

06:20 · 1:50 · 275 w
Screen
Voice over

Targets tab, click New target. Fill name, kind, category, easting, northing, TVDSS and radius.

A trajectory needs somewhere to go. On the Targets tab, a new target takes a name, a position and a size. A circle of a hundred and twenty metres radius at two thousand four hundred and seventy metres true vertical depth subsea is a perfectly ordinary one.

Click From registry. Choose a formation top, then a mapped surface.

Or take one from the registry. From registry builds a target from a formation top you picked back in the correlation episode, or from a surface you gridded in the mapping episode. Your geoscience becomes a drilling target without anybody retyping a coordinate.

Point at the easting and northing fields, then at the plan view showing the target relative to the wellhead.

One convention to know. Target eastings and northings are absolute coordinates in the site's system, the same frame as the wellhead. The plan view and the solvers convert them into offsets from the wellhead for you, in the wellbore's depth unit. So you type where the target is, not how far it is from the well.

Create a target with an implausible coordinate. Hold on the call-out on the Design tab.

And when that goes wrong, the app notices. A target more than fifty kilometres from the wellhead, or a wellbore with no wellhead location at all, is called out on the Design tab and in the solver dialog rather than being quietly drawn somewhere absurd or solved to.

05

The solvers

08:10 · 3:03 · 450 w
Screen
Voice over

Design tab, open Design methods. Hold so all five are readable.

Now the trajectory itself. Design methods holds five solvers, and they fall into two groups.

Point at the mode column: replace against append.

Replace methods rebuild the whole segment list from surface. Append methods continue from wherever your current design ends. Which means you can chain them. Solve the upper hole with one method, then append a landing with another.

Choose Build and hold (J). Pick the target, set a kickoff depth and a build rate. Click Solve.

Build and hold is the one to start with. Vertical to your kickoff, one build at the rate you choose, then a straight hold that lands on the target. Pick the target, type the kickoff and the rate, and solve.

Pause 3 seconds. The solve runs and the charts and KPI strip update.

KPI strip. Point at total MD, TVD at TD, displacement, max inclination, max dogleg severity, bottom hole.

The strip along the top gives you the shape of what you just made. Total measured depth. True vertical depth at total depth. Displacement. Maximum inclination. Maximum dogleg severity. And the bottom hole position.

Open the segments list. Edit one segment by hand and watch the charts follow.

Underneath, the segments the solver produced, and you can edit them by hand. The solver gives you a starting point, not a cage.

Show the other four methods in the dropdown, pausing on each.

The others each answer a different question. The S-profile builds, holds and then drops toward a final inclination while still hitting the target. Curve to target throws a single arc from wherever you are to wherever the target is, and reports the dogleg severity that would require, which is often the fastest way to find out whether an idea is drillable. Nudge is a shallow deviation and return, for getting away from your neighbours on a crowded pad.

Choose Horizontal landing. Select a heel target, then a toe target. Hold on the landing attitude the dialog displays.

And horizontal landing is the interesting one. It takes two targets. The heel is where the well lands. The toe sets the direction the lateral will run. Give it both and the landing azimuth and inclination are worked out from the heel to toe direction, and the dialog shows you the attitude it is about to solve with before you commit.

Point at the landing inclination field, left blank. Then type a value to override.

Note that the landing inclination is a field of its own, because horizontal wells are not all flat. A lateral planned to follow reservoir dip lands at eighty nine or ninety one degrees rather than ninety. Leave the field blank and it comes from your heel to toe line. A toe twenty metres deeper than the heel across four hundred metres of lateral lands the well at about eighty seven degrees, so it runs straight down that line. Type a value and you force the attitude instead.

06

Reading the design, and what Save means

11:13 · 2:00 · 300 w
Screen
Voice over

Design tab. Switch through the four views: Section, Plots, Survey, 3D.

Four views on the design. Section gives you true vertical depth against vertical section. Plots gives you plan, section, inclination and dogleg severity together. Survey is the full station table. And 3D is the pad itself.

Plan view. Point at the equal aspect, the slots, the lease lines, the targets in true shape and the north arrow.

The plan view is a true map with equal aspect, so north is never stretched against east. Your slots, your lease lines, your targets in their real shape and a north arrow, all where they actually are.

3D view. Orbit by dragging, shift-drag to pan, wheel to zoom. Change the vertical exaggeration. Toggle the layer buttons.

The three dimensional view puts the whole pad in a cube. Your plan, the other wellbores' designs, the targets and the registry tops. Drag to orbit, shift and drag to pan, the wheel zooms, and the vertical exaggeration selector stretches depth when a shallow pad would otherwise look flat.

Design Settings, change the survey listing interval, and show the station table getting denser while the KPI strip stays identical.

One control that confuses people. The survey listing interval controls how often a station is written into the table. It does not change the trajectory at all. A denser listing is a longer table describing the same well.

Click Save. Hold on the confirmation.

And now the most important sentence in this episode. Press Save, and the segments and the computed stations are written down. Publishing, the exports, the reports and the anti-collision scan all read that saved trajectory. Never the editor you are looking at.

Change a segment without saving, then open the export menu, to show it exporting the saved version.

So an unsaved change is invisible to everything downstream, deliberately. If you have edited a design and your report does not match, that is not a bug. That is the application refusing to publish work you have not committed to.

Break: end of Part 1 Recommended. Part 1 runs 13:13 and designs the well. Part 2 is about how wrong it might be and who else is down there, which is a different conversation. Part 2 opens on section 07 with a ten second recap of the saved design.
07

How wrong the line might be

13:13 · 2:20 · 350 w
Screen
Voice over

Toggle EOU on. The ellipses appear on the plan view, the band on the section, rings in 3D.

Toggle the uncertainty on, and your line grows a shape around it. Ellipses on the plan view, a band on the section, rings in the cube.

Zoom an ellipse near total depth, then one near surface, showing the growth.

Those are two standard deviations of positional uncertainty at each station, and notice how they grow with depth. Near surface you know almost exactly where you are. Three kilometres of hole later, you know rather less.

Open the survey program editor. Show instruments assigned to measured depth intervals tiling surface to total depth.

Where those come from is the error model, and it is the industry one. Twenty seven separate error sources, from depth measurement through the accelerometers and magnetometers to the geomagnetic reference, drillstring interference, sag and misalignment, propagated through your survey geometry into a covariance at every station.

Assign a different instrument to a deeper interval. Show the intervals tiling with no gap.

You control it through the survey program. Assign instruments to measured depth intervals, and those intervals have to tile the well from surface to total depth with no gaps. At a tool change the accumulated uncertainty freezes and the new tool starts growing from that tie-on point, which is the convention the standard specifies.

Select a wellbore with no cached magnetic model. Hold on the message naming what is missing.

The model needs your wellbore's cached magnetic values, or a coordinate system it can look them up in. Without either, the uncertainty and the anti-collision both state exactly what is missing instead of quietly running on defaults.

Hold on the ellipses again, wide.

And the implementation reproduces the standard's own published example well to numerical precision. That matters, because the next section is going to use these numbers to decide whether it is safe to drill.

08

Anti-collision

15:33 · 2:48 · 420 w
Screen
Voice over

Anti-Collision tab. Pick the reference trajectory, then tick two offsets.

Pick your reference trajectory, either the plan or the actual composite, and tick the offsets to scan against. Other wellbores' designs, and registry wells in the same coordinate system.

Show the separation rule panel with its parameters and defaults.

Then the rule. This is the industry separation factor, and the panel shows you every number in it rather than burying them. How many combined standard deviations of clearance you demand, three point five by default. A bit position allowance ahead of the last survey, half a metre. A fixed surface margin for the things nobody models, zero point three metres. The physical hole radii, because the rule is edge to edge and not centre to centre. And your own action thresholds for no-go and review.

Click Run.

Run it.

Pause 4 seconds. The scan runs across every station against every offset.

Status cards, then the separation factor ladder. Point at the minimum.

For every station on your reference, the engine finds the exact closest point on each offset, on the arcs themselves rather than only at the stations, projects both wells' uncertainty onto the line between their centres, and computes a separation factor. The ladder shows it down the whole well, and the minimum is the number people will ask you for.

Switch the ladder to distance mode. Point at the minimum allowable separation distance.

Switch it to distance and you get the same story in metres, including the distance at which the separation factor would fall to one, which is the number a directional driller can actually steer against.

Show the travelling cylinder in highside, then north. Then the violations table.

The travelling cylinder puts the offsets around your well as you look down it, in highside or in north. And the violations table lists everything that broke your thresholds.

Point back at the uncertainty ellipses, then at the separation factor.

Here is the part worth appreciating. Those are the same covariances that drew the ellipses a minute ago. The picture and the rule cannot disagree with each other, because they are the same numbers.

Click Save run. Change the plan, return to the tab and show the saved run unchanged.

Then Save run. That stores the scan on the design, immutably. It is your evidence that the plan was checked, it is the input to the report, and it can be read back later exactly as it was computed. Change the plan and the saved run does not follow. It never auto-updates, because a record of what you checked is worthless if it silently changes. Rerun and save again.

Actual surveys

18:21 · 1:40 · 250 w
Screen
Voice over

Surveys tab, click New survey. Show the three sources.

A plan stops being a plan the moment somebody drills it. The Surveys tab holds what actually happened.

Paste measured depth, inclination and azimuth lines. Introduce one bad line and hold on the numbered error.

You can paste stations straight in, three columns, and the validation is strict and tells you which line is wrong rather than which field.

Import from a file. Choose the Excel workbook, pick a sheet, hold on the preview, then map the three columns.

Or import a file. Delimited text with the delimiter you choose or detected for you, or an Excel workbook where you pick the sheet. Either way you get a preview of the first parsed rows before you map anything, so you can see how the file actually split before you tell it which column is measured depth.

Import from a registry well's deviation survey instead.

Or take the deviation survey straight from a registry well, which is where the surveys you loaded in the very first episode have been sitting all along.

Show the actual composite plotted against the plan on the section view.

And each run carries its own azimuth reference, because the tool that recorded it did. The composite of your runs becomes a trajectory in its own right, which you can plot against the plan and scan for collisions exactly like one.

Publish, and the mud window

20:01 · 2:40 · 400 w
Screen
Voice over

Click Publish. The dialog opens. Publish the plan.

Publish turns your saved trajectory into a registry well. The first publish creates it and bridges it to this wellbore. Every publish after that updates the same registry row in place, so anything already pointing at this well keeps working.

Point at the checkshot borrow option in the dialog.

There is an option to borrow a nearby well's checkshots, which gives your plan a time to depth relationship and lets it hang correctly in a seismic display before a single metre has been drilled.

Cut to Seismolord showing the planned well in the cube, then Well Data Manager listing it.

And now your plan is a well like any other. Seismolord draws it in the cube and on sections with your seismic. Well Data Manager lists it. Correlation and Petrophysics will take tops and logs on it.

Cut to Pore Pressure Studio, select the bridged well, and publish the prognosis onto it.

Including Pore Pressure Studio. Point last episode's prognosis at this well and publish it.

Back in Well Design Studio, Section view, toggle PPFG on. Hold as the curves appear along the trajectory.

Then come back here, open the Section view, and turn on the pressure display.

Hold wide on the trajectory with the shaded safe window along it. Then zoom the tightest point.

There it is. The pore pressure and the fracture gradient, hanging on your trajectory, converted from depth to your measured depth exactly through the well path you designed. The safe window is shaded, and the tightest point on it is called out. That is the mud weight you can hold and the mud weight that breaks the formation, along the actual hole you are planning to drill, rather than along a vertical line somebody assumed.

Switch the display between megapascals and equivalent mud weight.

Read it in megapascals or as an equivalent mud weight, whichever your casing design speaks. And if any of the prerequisites are missing, the panel says exactly which one rather than showing you an empty chart.

Exports and the report pack

22:41 · 1:50 · 275 w
Screen
Voice over

Open the export menu. Hold so all four entries are readable.

Four exports, and they are for four different audiences.

Click the quick survey export, then the trajectory contract. Open the JSON and scroll its header.

The quick survey file is the listing you are looking at, in your unit, for somebody who wants a table. The trajectory contract is the serious one. That is what you feed to torque and drag, to casing design, or to a tool outside this Suite.

Show the trajectory CSV and Excel outputs, then the DXF opened in a CAD viewer if possible.

The same stations come out as a spreadsheet if a person needs to read them. And the D. X. F. gives you the wellpath as a CAD polyline in site coordinates, elevation positive up, for whoever is drawing the pad.

Reports tab. Generate the wall plot and hold on it.

Then the report pack. The wall plot is the one people pin up. Header block, plan and section drawn as vectors with the uncertainty on them, the key stations and the targets, on a single landscape page.

Generate the survey listing, then the anti-collision report from the saved run.

The survey listing is the full table with its depth summary. And the anti-collision report is built from a saved run, with the parameters you used, the minimum separation factor per offset, the ladder and the violations. All three render from the saved trajectory, in your browser, so nothing leaves for a server to make a picture.

Wrap up

Screen
Voice over

Pull back: the 3D pad with the plan, the uncertainty rings, the offsets and the targets.

So that is Well Design Studio. A site, a wellbore that knows which north it is speaking, a trajectory solved to a target, an uncertainty model on every station, a separation scan saved as evidence, and a mud window hanging on the path you actually designed.
