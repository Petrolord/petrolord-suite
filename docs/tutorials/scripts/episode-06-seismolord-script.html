Before you record

  1. Publish this one as two parts. Twenty six minutes is past what anybody watches in a sitting, and unlike the earlier episodes the two halves are genuinely different subjects. Part 1 is the seismic. Part 2 is tying it to the wells and getting a map out. The break is marked after section 06 and both chapter lists are below.
  2. Use a small survey. Section 02 imports a volume on camera and the decode has to finish inside the take. Pick the smallest volume that still looks like real data, and have a larger one already imported for everything after that.
  3. You need a well with sonic and density inside the survey. Sections 07 and 08 are the heart of the episode and neither works without those two logs. If that well already carries imported checkshots, better still, because section 08 shows the tie storing a separate derived set rather than overwriting them.
  4. Choose your reflector before the take, and know its polarity. Section 04 sets the snap event to peak, trough or zero crossing before placing a single seed. Deciding that on camera looks like guessing.
  5. Interpret at least one fault beforehand. Section 05 is short by design, and sections 04 and 10 both refer back to a fault existing. Picking a whole fault on camera would cost four minutes for very little.
The argument this episode is making. A tie is a proposal, not an overwrite. Section 07 says every anchor is an assertion that one event on the log is one event on the seismic. Section 08 shows the commit writing a derived checkshot set while the imported one stays untouched, and Well Data Manager saying plainly which is in charge. Section 12 sends the viewer to check the horizon against the log top. Those three carry the episode.
Second-strongest beat. Section 02, where the import scan reads the traces rather than believing the textual header. It is a one-sentence idea that tells an experienced interpreter this application was built by people who have been burned before. Give it room.

Before the take

Top Ekene Sand is a PEAK under SEG normal polarity: the reservoir is faster and denser than the overpressured shale above it. Set the snap event to peak before you place a seed. Tie on Ekene-1, which carries sonic, density and an imported checkshot set. Pick the growth fault at the Oboro or Akata level, where it has real throw; it dies out above the reservoir, which is exactly why the Ekene tank is unfaulted.

A well tells you the truth about one place. Seismic tells you something about everywhere, and it tells you in time rather than in depth. Seismolord is where those two facts get reconciled.

02

Importing a volume

01:32 · 2:55 · 430 w
Screen
Voice over

Explorer, click Import SEG-Y volume. Choose the small survey file.

In the explorer on the left, Import SEG-Y volume. Choose your file, and the first thing that happens is a scan.

The scan result: proposed inline, crossline and coordinate byte positions, the sample format, the coordinate scalar, and the measured geometry. Track down the panel.

The scan reads the file's headers, and then it does something more important. It reads the traces themselves and works out where the inline number, the crossline number and the coordinates actually live in each trace header. It proposes the byte positions, tells you whether the samples are I. B. M. floats or I triple E floats, finds the coordinate scalar, and shows you the measured geometry of the survey.

Point at the textual header preview, then back at the measured geometry.

And that ordering is deliberate. The textual header at the front of a SEG-Y file is a note somebody typed, and notes are wrong all the time. The traces are the data. So the header is offered as a hint, and the geometry you see is measured. Confirm the proposal when it looks right, and change it when it does not.

The CRS step. Declare the coordinate reference system. If you can produce it, show the area-of-use warning on a deliberately wrong declaration, then correct it.

Then declare the coordinate reference system. Every import door in Geoscience asks for this, and this one checks your answer against the coordinates in the file. If you declare a system the survey could not plausibly sit in, it says so before anything gets written.

Start the import. The volume appears in the explorer as decoding; the storage meter moves.

Then let it run. The decode happens in a background worker, streaming the volume into your private storage as small bricks, so nothing ever loads the whole file into memory. You can carry on working while it goes, and the storage meter in the explorer shows what your account is holding.

Pause 3 seconds. Let the decode progress be visible. Cut forward in the edit if it takes longer.

Point at the Project CRS chip in the status bar.

One thing to check before you move on. The project coordinate system chip in the status bar. Everything in Geoscience is stored in that one system, and a volume imported under an unknown system cannot be mapped against your wells. If that chip is amber, fix it now rather than wondering later why nothing lines up.

03

Moving through the cube

04:27 · 2:52 · 430 w
Screen
Voice over

Home tab, Volume group. Select the volume. A section draws.

Select the volume in the Home tab and a section appears.

Line group. Step inlines with the slider, then with the wheel. Switch orientation to crossline, then to time slice. Keep the status bar readout in frame.

The Line group drives where you are. Orientation picks inline, crossline, time slice or a traverse. Step through lines with the slider or with the wheel. The status bar tells you which line you are on, and reads out the inline, the crossline, the time and the amplitude under your cursor as you move.

Display group. Change the colormap, then reverse it.

The Display group is the picture. The colormap, and a reverse toggle. The defaults follow the conventions you expect. Normal polarity, and a colour bar symmetric about zero, so zero amplitude sits in the middle of the scale rather than somewhere arbitrary.

Scaling group. Cycle the amplitude scale through None, Percentile, RMS multiple and Manual clip, holding on each.

The Scaling group decides how amplitudes map to colour. None shows raw values. Percentile clips at a percentile of the data you are looking at, which is the safe everyday choice. R. M. S. multiple scales by the energy in a window. And manual clip is for when you want an exact number and no surprises.

Trace style: switch from density to wiggle with variable area over density, and back.

Trace style switches between a density display, wiggle traces with variable area, or both together, which is what most people want when they are picking.

Adjust gain, then automatic gain control. Hold on the changing display.

And here is a guarantee worth stating. Gain and automatic gain control are applied in the display only. They never touch the stored amplitudes. Whatever you do to make the picture readable, the numbers underneath it are still the numbers that came out of the file.

Line group, Domain. Switch from time to depth. Hold. Switch back.

Domain switches a section from two way time into depth, through the volume's velocity model. Read that as a display stretch, because that is exactly what it is. Your picks stay in time. The depth view is a way of looking, not a conversion of your interpretation.

Co-render group. Add a second volume as an overlay. Set blend to opacity mix, then to multiply.

And if you have a second volume, the Co-render group paints it over the first. Opacity mix lays the overlay on top. Multiply darkens the seismic by it, which is the one you want for a variance or coherence cube.

Picking a horizon

07:19 · 3:12 · 470 w
Screen
Voice over

Explorer, create a new horizon and name it. Switch to the Interpretation tab.

Now the interpretation itself. Make a new horizon, and switch to the Interpretation tab.

Snap group. Open the Event selector showing peak, trough and zero crossing. Choose the one your reflector is.

Before you pick anything, set the Snap group. Event decides what your clicks land on. A peak, a trough, or a zero crossing. Set it to whatever your marker actually is, and every seed you place will snap to the nearest one instead of landing wherever your mouse happened to stop.

Click three or four seeds along the chosen reflector on the section.

Then click your seeds along the event. Three or four on a clear section is plenty to start with.

Seed & track group. Click Track 2D. The pick extends along the line. Hold on where it stops.

Track 2D follows that event along the line you are on. Watch where it goes, and more importantly watch where it stops. A tracker that runs out of confidence and halts is telling you something about the data.

Click Track 3D. Tracking progress appears in the status bar.

Track 3D grows the pick out through the whole cube from your seeds, within the correlation and jump limits set beside the buttons. The status bar reports its progress, and Cancel stops it.

Pause 4 seconds. The volume track runs. Then step to an inline never visited in this take.

Step to a distant inline and hold on the tracked pick there.

Then step to an inline you have never visited and see whether the tracker agrees with you there. That is the test, not whether it looked good on the line you seeded.

Turn on Stop at faults in the Interpretation tab. Re-run a track near the interpreted fault.

Stop at faults is the toggle that keeps a tracker honest. With faults interpreted and that switch on, the tracker will not jump across a fault and carry your horizon into the wrong block.

Edit horizon group. Brush a stretch of pick back into place. Then Undo in the Home tab, then Redo.

Where the tracker is wrong, the Edit horizon group lets you brush the pick back into place. And every pick, every track and every edit is on the undo stack. Undo and redo live in the Home tab and they cover all of it.

Surface ops group. Smooth the horizon, then Fill holes. Then expand the horizon in the explorer to show its version chain and compare two versions.

Surface ops cleans up afterwards. Smooth takes the noise off a tracked pick, and fill holes closes the gaps the tracker left. And every horizon keeps its versions. The explorer shows the chain, and you can display an older version, compare it with the current one, or restore it. So an aggressive autotrack is never a decision you cannot take back.

07

The synthetic and the tie

13:09 · 3:04 · 460 w
Screen
Voice over

Wells tab, click Synthetics. The panel opens on the chosen well.

Here is the heart of the episode. A seismic volume is in time. A well is in depth. Neither is any use to the other until you know how to get between them, and a synthetic seismogram is how you find out.

The synthetics panel with the sonic and density curves listed and drawn.

The Synthetics panel takes the sonic and the density logs from the well, multiplies them to get acoustic impedance, differences that to get a reflection coefficient at every boundary, and convolves the result with a wavelet. What comes out is what the seismic ought to look like at that well, if your logs and your wavelet are right.

Wavelet controls. Show Ricker with its frequency, then switch to the extracted option and back.

Choose the wavelet. A Ricker at a frequency you pick is the honest starting point. Or extract one from the seismic itself, which is better when the data supports it.

The tie window: the synthetic track beside the real seismic track at the well. Hold.

Then the tie window puts your synthetic beside the real seismic at the well, and the job is to make them line up.

Double-click on the tracks to drop an anchor. Drag its diamond to stretch. Double-click an anchor to remove it. Do all three slowly.

Double click to drop an anchor. Drag its diamond to stretch or squeeze the synthetic against the seismic. Double click an anchor again to remove it. You are not moving the seismic and you are not moving the well. You are adjusting the relationship between time and depth until the two pictures agree.

Zoom the tie quality readout showing mean and minimum correlation. Keep it in frame while adjusting an anchor.

Watch the quality readout while you work. It gives you the mean correlation between the synthetic and the trace, and the worst correlation in the window. A good mean with a poor minimum means you have tied the top of the section beautifully and left the bottom of it a mess, which is worth knowing before you commit to anything.

Settle on a tie with a small number of anchors. Hold on it.

And be careful how hard you pull. Every anchor you add is an assertion that a particular event on the log is a particular event on the seismic. A tie with three anchors you believe is worth far more than a tie with fifteen that you forced.

Committing the tie

16:13 · 2:12 · 330 w
Screen
Voice over

Click Commit to checkshots. Hold on the confirmation.

When the tie is right, commit it. Commit to checkshots stores the warp you just made as a checkshot set on that well.

Hover the button so its tooltip is readable, then hold on the wording about imported checkshots never being overwritten.

And here is the design decision worth pausing on. It does not overwrite the checkshots that came with the well. It stores a separate, derived set, and it marks it as derived. Your original survey data is still exactly where it was.

Cut to Well Data Manager, the same well, Checkshots tab. Zoom the amber note about the tie-derived set being in charge.

Go over to Well Data Manager and look at that well's checkshots tab, and it tells you. It says a tie-derived time depth set is in charge for this well, and that edits made there will apply once that set is cleared in Seismolord. Two applications, one well, and neither of them quietly overruling the other.

Back in Seismolord, point at Clear derived checkshots without clicking.

Clear derived checkshots reverts it, and the well goes back to the checkshots it was imported with. So the tie is a proposal you can withdraw, not a change you have to live with.

Click Calibrate velocity. Then switch a section to the depth domain to show the effect.

The other button does something different. Calibrate velocity fits the volume's velocity model to your anchors and saves it with a record that it came from this tie. That is what makes the depth domain we looked at earlier mean something, and it is what lets the map show depth instead of time.

Layer selector: choose the horizon, then a registry surface from Episode 4.

The layer selector chooses what is mapped. Your horizon, or any surface in the registry, including the ones we gridded in Mapping and Surface Studio two episodes ago. They are drawn on the same lattice, so you can compare them directly.

Domain selector: switch from two way time to depth. Hold on the relabelled colour bar.

The domain selector switches between two way time and depth. Depth needs a velocity model, which is exactly what the calibration we just did provided.

Attribute selector: switch from structure to amplitude. Set the extraction half-window. Hold on the amplitude map.

And the attribute selector changes what the colour means. Structure is the depth or the time of the horizon itself. Switch it to amplitude and the app extracts the seismic amplitude along your pick, in a window you set, and maps that instead. That is where a bright zone or a flat spot shows itself as a map, rather than as something you noticed on one line and hoped was real.

Turn on the wells, faults and culture layers. Then contours and annotations. Then the snapshot button.

Wells, faults, registry surfaces and culture layers all overlay. Contours and labels come from the same kind of controls you used in Mapping and Surface Studio, and the camera button saves a snapshot.

Draw a traverse across the map: click a path, double-click to finish. The Traverse window opens with the section along it.

One more thing worth knowing here. Draw a traverse on the map, clicking a path across the survey and double clicking to finish, and a section along that exact path opens in its own window. That is how you make a line through three wells that the acquisition never actually shot.

Voice over

Export tab, click Export. The panel opens.

Your horizon is picks on a lattice. To be useful to anybody else it has to become a surface, and the Export tab is where that happens.

Choose the horizon, then show the output options: horizon picks, gridded surface, gridded amplitude map.

Pick your horizon, then say what you want out. The raw picks. A gridded surface. Or a gridded amplitude map, which is the attribute we just looked at, turned into a surface of its own.

Set the cell size. Turn on fault blocking and hover its tooltip.

Set a cell size, and notice the fault option. With it on, the interpolation will not cross a fault that cuts this horizon, so a throw stays a throw instead of being smoothed into a gentle ramp. The nodes on the fault trace stay null.

Domain and sign convention. Show two way time, then depth, then toggle between Negative down (suite) and Positive down (Petrel).

Then the domain and the sign convention, and this one deserves your full attention. Time surfaces go out as positive two way time in milliseconds. Depth surfaces go out as elevations, negative below datum, which is the convention this whole Suite uses. And because the rest of the industry is not always so consistent, there is a Petrel option that writes positive downward instead. Choose the one your recipient expects, and tell them which you chose when you send it.

Choose the registry destination and publish. Cut to Mapping and Surface Studio with the surface now in its list, then to ReservoirCalc Pro's surface import.

Now the destination. Save it to the registry and it becomes a first class surface, listed in Mapping and Surface Studio, in Earth Modeling, and in ReservoirCalc Pro's surface import. The map you just made from seismic goes straight into the volumetrics we did last episode.

Back in the export panel, type a contact into the contact field and hold on the gross rock volume readout.

There is even a gross rock volume readout in this panel. Type a contact and it gives you a number on the spot. Treat it as a sanity check, exactly as in the mapping application.

Show the file format list, then hover the Import button beside Export.

Or export to a file, in the same industry formats you have seen throughout this series. And the Import button beside it reads the same dialects back, so picks and surfaces from another package arrive here as first class objects rather than as pictures.
