Before you record

  1. You need one top on six to eight wells, well spread. Three is the minimum the engine accepts and it makes an ugly map. The kriging beats in section 06 need enough points for a variogram fit to mean anything, so this is the setup step that decides whether the episode looks convincing.
  2. Grid and publish a second, deeper surface before the take. Section 08 computes an isochore, which needs a top surface and a base surface already in the list. Doing that on camera would add four minutes of repetition.
  3. Have a grid file ready to import. Section 09 imports one. Any of the four formats will do. If you have a file that came from Petrel, use it, because the format detection is more impressive on somebody else's export than on your own.
  4. Render section by section, honour the pauses. Same as every episode. Copy each section into the voice tool separately, and put real silence where the script marks a pause, because those are the moments the engine is gridding.
The argument this episode is making. A map is an interpretation, so it has to be reproducible. Stated in section 01, paid off in section 05 when publishing records the whole recipe, defended in section 07 when hand editing a contour leaves a listed guide point behind rather than an invisible smudge, and closed in section 11. Those are the beats to protect.
This one runs long too. There is a marked break after section 05, where the viewer has made and published their first map. Cut there for two parts of roughly ten and thirteen minutes. Chapter lists are given for both.

01

A map is an interpretation

00:00 · 1:20 · 200 w
Screen
Voice over

Dashboard, Geoscience module. Hold on the Mapping & Surface Studio tile, then open it.

Three wells give you three numbers. A map claims to know the depth everywhere in between. That claim is an interpretation, and this application is built around treating it like one.

A finished structure map: contoured, coloured, wells posted, legend and scale bar on. Hold for four seconds.

It takes the tops you picked in Well Correlation, or the zone properties you computed in Petrophysics Studio, and grids them into a surface. Then it lets you argue with that surface. Change the engine. Split it across a fault. Clip it to a boundary. Push a contour where the seismic says it should go. And when you publish, it records every one of those decisions, so the map can be rebuilt exactly, by you or by anybody else.

Quick cuts, two seconds each: the grid form, a contour map drawing, a fault block being outlined, ReservoirCalc Pro listing the surface in its import.

In this video we will grid a top into a structure map, shape it, measure a rock volume from it, and hand it on to the applications that need it. Let us make a map.

02

Grid your first surface

01:20 · 2:55 · 430 w
Screen
Voice over

Left explorer, the block headed Grid a new surface. Open the source dropdown so all three groups are readable.

Everything starts at the top of the explorer, in the block headed Grid a new surface. The first dropdown asks what you are mapping, and it offers three kinds of thing.

Point at each group heading in the open dropdown in turn.

Structure, which is a top across your wells. Attribute, which is a zone property such as average porosity or net to gross. Or stratigraphy, which measures between two tops to give you gross thickness, net sand thickness, or net to gross. Everything in that list is already in the registry. You are not importing anything to get started.

Choose the top. The depth reference dropdown appears below. Open it, then close it on TVDSS.

Choose a top, and a second dropdown appears asking for the depth reference. This matters more than any other choice on the panel, so we will come back to it properly in a moment. For now leave it on true vertical depth subsea, which is the default and almost always the right answer.

Leave the method on the thin-plate spline. Type a cell size into the cell m box. Click Grid.

Below that is the gridding method, which we will also come back to. Then the cell size in metres, and the Grid button. Cell size is a judgment. Too coarse and you lose the shape. Too fine and you are drawing detail your wells cannot support. Something around a tenth of your typical well spacing is a sensible place to start.

Pause 3 seconds. Gridding runs and the map draws.

Zoom the status bar so the whole grid report is legible. Track along it slowly.

The map appears, and the status bar gives you a report. Read it properly, because it is the most useful sentence in this application.

Point at each clause of the report in turn.

It names the surface and the depth reference it used, along with the display unit. It says how many wells contributed and how many nodes the grid has. If a well could not be placed, it names that well and says why. And if any top sits below the last station of its survey, so its position had to follow the final tangent of the borehole, it counts those too.

Hold on the map. Point at a well that carries the top, then at one in the explorer well list that does not.

One thing the report deliberately does not complain about. A well that simply does not carry this top is not a problem. It is just a well without that pick, so it is not mentioned. What does get reported is a well that has the top and could not be placed, which is a real problem and worth your attention.

03

Elevation, negative down

04:15 · 2:00 · 300 w
Screen
Voice over

Zoom the contour labels so the minus signs are unmistakable.

Now look at the numbers on those contours. They are negative. That is not a bug, and it is worth two minutes of your life to understand, because it is where most mapping mistakes begin.

Point at the status bar text reading elevation, negative down.

Every depth surface in the Suite is stored as an elevation, measured from the datum, positive up and negative down. So a surface at minus two thousand one hundred is deeper than one at minus two thousand. The status bar states the convention at all times, so you never have to guess which way round you are.

Ribbon, click the depth: button to switch to metres. Let the contour labels and colour bar relabel. Switch back.

The button on the ribbon sets the display unit. It changes what you see and what you type. The contour interval, the colour bar, the posted values, the guide values, the contact you type for a volume, and the unit of anything you export. Storage never changes. It is metres underneath, whatever you are looking at.

Back on the grid form, open the depth reference dropdown and hold so all three options are readable.

And back on the grid form, the depth reference decides what is being gridded in the first place. True vertical depth subsea puts every well on the same datum, and that is what a structure map means. True vertical depth measures below each well's kelly bushing, so wells with different rig floor elevations stop agreeing with each other. Measured depth is the raw logged depth, which on a deviated well is not a map depth at all. It is there for comparing against a legacy map, and for nothing else.

The map window

Wheel zoom in, drag to pan, double click to fit.

The map window behaves the way you expect. Wheel to zoom, drag to pan, double click to fit the whole surface back into the window.

Move the cursor across the map and hold on a well, so the readout at the bottom right is legible.

Hover anywhere and the readout at the bottom right gives you the X, the Y, and the value of the surface under your cursor, in the display unit. That is your fastest sanity check. Put the cursor on a well and see whether the surface agrees with the well.

Dock, Display. Type a contour interval, hold, then clear it back to blank.

The Display block is where the picture gets set. Contour interval takes a number in your display unit, and leaving it blank picks an interval that gives you about ten levels, which is usually right.

Change the colour map, then tick Reverse colours.

Colour map offers the structure ramp, where shallow is warm, plus any shared colour map, and a reverse toggle for when your audience expects the other convention.

Toggle each in turn, leaving a beat on each: Contour labels, Well names, Posted values, Legend, Scale bar, North arrow, Axes.

Then a row of toggles for everything that makes a map a map. Contour labels. Well names. Posted values, which is the actual control value at each well. A legend, a scale bar, a north arrow, and the axes. Leave posted values on while you are working, because a map that does not honour its own control points is telling you something.

Ribbon, click PNG. Open the downloaded image full screen and hold.

And P. N. G. on the ribbon gives you the map as a titled, captioned image at twice your screen resolution, ready to drop into a report.

05

Publish, and what it records

08:19 · 1:26 · 215 w
Screen
Voice over

Point at the status bar reading unsaved preview. Then click Publish surface on the ribbon.

Until you publish, what you are looking at is a preview, and the status bar says so. Click Publish surface and it becomes a registry surface, with a name and a row in the list below.

Hover the new surface row so its tooltip describing the source appears. Hold long enough to read it.

And here is why that matters more than a save button usually does. A published surface records how it was made. Its source, its depth reference, its cell size, the control points with their posted values, the wells that were skipped, any fault blocks and boundary you used, any guide points you placed, and your display settings.

Right-click the surface and hover Re-grid without clicking. Close the menu.

Which means the map is reproducible. Anybody can see what went into it. And when a well gets a new pick six months from now, you can re-grid the surface from its own recorded recipe, rather than trying to remember what you did on a Thursday afternoon.

06

Spline or kriging

09:45 · 2:44 · 410 w
Screen
Voice over

Grid form. Open the method dropdown so both options are readable.

Back to the choice we skipped. The gridding method. There are two, and they answer different questions.

Grid with the spline. Turn on posted values, then hover a well so the readout and the posted value can be seen matching.

The thin-plate spline is the default. It passes exactly through every control point. Put the cursor on a well and the surface reads the well's value, precisely. That is what you want when you trust your picks and you want the smoothest surface that honours all of them.

Switch the method to kriging. The variogram panel appears below it.

Ordinary kriging asks a different question. Instead of insisting on the wells, it weights them by how far apart they are and by how quickly the surface stops correlating with distance. That relationship is the variogram, and the panel that just appeared is where you set it.

Point at the model selector, then the range, sill and nugget boxes in turn. Then click Fit and let the numbers populate.

Choose a model. Spherical, exponential or gaussian. Then three numbers. The range, in metres, is the distance beyond which two wells no longer tell you anything about each other. The sill is the variance of the field. The nugget is the noise you believe sits in the measurements at zero distance. And if you would rather not guess, the Fit button reads the range and the sill straight off the experimental variogram of your own control points.

Point at remove the trend first, already ticked. Click Grid.

The remove the trend first tick is worth understanding. It fits a plane through your wells, kriges what is left over, then puts the plane back. On a field with a consistent regional dip, that is the difference between a sensible map and a bullseye around every well.

Tick show the variance map. The display swaps. Hold on it, pointing at a well-controlled area then at an empty flank. Untick.

And kriging gives you something the spline cannot. A variance map. Tick show the variance map and the display swaps to it. Low where your wells constrain the answer. High where the surface is a guess. Show that map to anyone who asks how confident you are about the flank of a structure. It is a far more honest answer than a contour.

07

Shaping the map

12:29 · 2:52 · 430 w
Screen
Voice over

Dock, Polygons. Click Fault block, click vertices around a block on the map, type a name, click Save.

A spline through scattered wells does not know about faults. So you tell it. Click Fault block, click your way around the block on the map, give it a name, and save it.

Tick the grid box beside the new fault polygon. Click Grid again. Hold on the discontinuity across the fault trace.

Then tick the grid box beside it and grid again. The surface is now built independently inside and outside that polygon, so it is allowed to be discontinuous across the fault, which is what faults actually do.

Click Boundary, draw the field outline, save it, then select its clip radio button and re-grid.

A Boundary polygon does the opposite job. Mark one as clip and gridding stops at its edge, so the map does not extrapolate confidently out into an area where you have no data at all.

Click Add a guide point, click a position on the map, type a value, click Add, then re-grid.

Guide points are for what you know that the wells do not. Click Add a guide point, click a position on the map, and type the value you believe belongs there. It grids alongside the wells as an extra control point. If seismic tells you the crest sits at a certain elevation and there is no well on it, that is exactly what a guide point is for.

Click Move a contour. Press on a contour, drag it to a new position, release. The surface re-grids through it.

And Move a contour is the same idea with a friendlier handle. Press on a contour, drag it to where you think it belongs, and release. The app turns that contour into guide points at its own value and re-grids the surface through them. It is hand editing, honestly labelled.

Scroll to the guide point list and show the points the contour drag created, each with its value. Hover Clear all guide points.

And that last part matters more than it sounds. Hand editing that leaves no trace is how maps become impossible to defend. Here, every hand edit becomes a listed point with a value and a position that you can see, question and remove, and one button clears the lot if you change your mind.

08

Arithmetic, and a quick volume

15:21 · 2:27 · 360 w
Screen
Voice over

Dock, Surface arithmetic. Open the operation dropdown so the whole list is readable.

Two surfaces are more useful than one. The Surface arithmetic block combines them.

Choose Isochore: top minus base. Pick the shallower surface as A and the deeper one as B. Click Compute isochore.

The one you will use most is the isochore. Choose your top surface and your base surface, and compute. Because both are elevations, subtracting them gives a thickness that is positive wherever the base is deeper, which is the right way round.

Pause 3 seconds. The isochore computes and draws as a thickness map.

Re-open the operation dropdown and point down the rest of the list. Then publish the isochore.

The rest of the list is what you would expect. Add and subtract. Multiply, which is how an isochore times a net to gross map becomes a net thickness map. The shallower or the deeper of two surfaces, for truncations. Add or multiply by a constant. And clip to a boundary. Anything you compute here is a preview until you publish it, exactly like a gridded surface.

Point at the small note under the compute button about resampling.

If the two surfaces sit on different grid frames, the second is resampled onto the first for you. You do not have to line them up yourself.

Dock, Quick GRV. Type a contact elevation as a negative number. Click GRV. Zoom the result line.

Underneath is quick gross rock volume. Type a contact as an elevation, remembering that it is negative below datum, press G. R. V., and you get the rock volume of the displayed structure above that contact, along with its area.

Point at the note beneath it naming ReservoirCalc Pro.

Treat that number as a read-out for a quick sanity check. It is deliberately not a volumetrics tool. Fluids, saturations and uncertainty belong in ReservoirCalc Pro, and the surface you just made is already available there.

09

Import, export, share, re-grid

17:48 · 2:20 · 350 w
Screen
Voice over

Explorer header, click Import. Choose the prepared grid file. The dialog opens with the format already detected.

Not every surface starts here. Import, in the explorer header, reads X. Y. Z. points, C. P. S. three, Zed map plus and Irap classic grids, and works out the format for you.

Point at Values are, then Depth unit in the file, then the sign convention control.

It asks you three things. What the values are, whether depth, two way time, or an attribute. The depth unit of the file. And the sign convention, which it guesses from the data and lets you override. Whichever way the file was written, a depth grid is stored as an elevation once it is in.

Point at the CRS declaration in the dialog, then complete the import.

You also declare the file's coordinate reference system. If it differs from your project's, and both are known, the grid is converted on the way in rather than landing in the wrong place.

Right-click a surface, hover the Export submenu showing the four formats, then hover the control points option.

Going the other way, right click any surface and Export offers those same four formats, written in your display unit. There is also a control points file, which gives you the well values the map was built from, and that is very often what somebody actually wanted.

Click the private badge on your own surface row. It turns to the shared badge.

Surfaces are private until you click the badge on the row. Shared surfaces are read only for everyone else in your organization, exactly like wells.

Right-click, choose Re-grid. The form fills from the surface and the row is marked. Point at Publish now reading Replace surface, then click it.

And Re-grid, from that same menu, sets the whole form back to the way that surface was made, and turns Publish into Replace surface. The replacement keeps the original identity, so anything already pointing at this surface follows it to the new version instead of breaking.
