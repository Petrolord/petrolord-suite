Before you record

  1. Save a section in Well Correlation first. The Section view opens whatever you last saved there. With nothing saved, sections 06 and 09 have nothing to show and section 07 has nothing to re-plot. This is the single most important preparation step in the episode.
  2. Date at least four tops, and make one an unconformity with a hiatus end age. The tract table in section 03, the Wheeler chart in section 07 and the age-depth plot in section 08 all need dated surfaces. The hiatus is what makes the best beat in section 07 work, so it has to exist.
  3. One well needs a lithology log. The net sand map in section 09 and the Basin handoff in section 08 both read it. A short one is fine.
  4. Have two or three core photographs ready with their depths, for section 05.
  5. Decide your terminology before the take. Set Terms to whichever school your audience was trained in and leave it there for the whole episode, switching only during section 04. Flipping it casually elsewhere will confuse anyone following along on their own screen.
The argument this episode is making. Separate what you observed from what you call it. Section 03 treats each surface type as a specific claim about what happened. Section 04 shows the terminology switch relabelling every application at once while changing nothing stored. Section 07 shows the Wheeler chart turning a line into a measurable gap. The closing beat ties the two together. Those are the beats to protect.
Check the name before you render anything. Catuneanu is a Romanian surname, roughly cuh-tuh-NYAH-noo, and it appears throughout. Say it once into your voice tool, listen, and fix the spelling in your paste before rendering the rest. Getting it wrong across a whole episode aimed at stratigraphers would undo a lot of goodwill.

01

Two schools, one dataset

00:00 · 1:48 · 270 w
Screen
Voice over

Dashboard, Geoscience module. Hold on the Stratigraphy Studio tile, then open it.

Sequence stratigraphy has a problem that has nothing to do with rocks. Two schools name the same surfaces differently, and have done for thirty years. Put an Exxon trained geologist and a Catuneanu trained one in a room with the same well, and they will agree about the geology and argue about the labels.

Ribbon, the Terms selector. Switch from Catuneanu to Exxon and back, letting the labels visibly change.

Stratigraphy Studio takes a position on that, and it is a good one. It stores one vocabulary and displays whichever one you want. What goes into the database never changes. What you and your colleague read on the screen is each of your own choosing.

Pan along the view bar: Column, Tops, Intervals, Core, Section, Wheeler, Ages, Glossary.

Beyond that, this is the specialist stratigrapher's home in the Suite. The stratigraphic column. Typed surfaces on the tops you have already picked. Lithology, core and facies logs. Core photographs. A Wheeler chart. An age-depth plot. And every one of those is a shared registry row, so what you write here appears in every other application straight away.

Quick cuts, two seconds each: a section with typed surfaces, the Wheeler chart, the age-depth plot.

In this video we will build a column, type the tops, log the rock, read a section as systems tracts, and then re-plot the whole thing against time. Let us start with the column.

The stratigraphic column

Screen
Voice over

Column view. Click Add unit, type a name, set a rank.

The Column view holds your stratigraphic framework, independent of any well. Add unit creates one. Give it a name and a rank, from the largest divisions down to the smallest.

Set a parent for the unit. Add two more inside it. Hold on the tree forming.

Then place it inside a parent, and the units build into a tree. A group holds formations, a formation holds members, and so on down.

Click From stage, choose a stage from the list. Both age fields fill.

Each unit carries a top age and a base age, in millions of years. You can type them, or press From stage and pick a stage off the international timescale, and both ages fill themselves.

Set the order among siblings, then the colour.

Units also carry an order among their siblings, youngest first, and a colour that will follow them everywhere else in the Suite.

Deliberately give a child an age range outside its parent's. Click Save column. Hold on the refusal naming the unit.

Now watch what happens when the framework does not hold together. Put a child outside its parent's age range, or give a unit a base younger than its top, and Save column refuses the whole save and names the unit that is wrong. It will not write half a framework and leave you to find the other half later.

Correct the ages and save successfully.

Fix it and save again.

Delete a unit that has children. Show the children remaining in the list with no parent.

And deleting is deliberately unexciting. Remove a unit and its children keep their rows with no parent, and any top that named it simply loses the reference. Nothing cascades, and nothing disappears behind your back.

03

Typing the tops

04:04 · 3:00 · 450 w
Screen
Voice over

Pick a well in the left explorer. The Tops view opens with its tops listed.

Pick a well on the left and open the Tops view. These are the same tops you picked in Well Correlation and in Petrophysics Studio. Stratigraphy Studio does not create new ones. It adds meaning to the ones you already have.

Open the surface type dropdown and hold so the whole list is readable.

The surface type is the first column, and this is the heart of it. Every top starts life as a formation top, which carries no sequence meaning at all. Change it, and you are making a statement.

Set one top to maximum flooding surface. Then show subaerial unconformity, maximum regressive surface and basal surface of forced regression on other tops.

A maximum flooding surface. A subaerial unconformity. A maximum regressive surface, marking the end of lowstand regression and the start of transgression. A basal surface of forced regression. A transgressive ravinement surface. Each one is a specific claim about what happened at that depth, and each one draws differently on every section in the Suite.

For one top, set the unit it tops, the confidence and the age.

Beside the type, say which unit this top is the top of, how confident you are, and the age if you know it.

On the unconformity, show the second age field appearing. Hover its tooltip so the wording is readable.

And when the surface is an unconformity, a second age field appears, asking when the hiatus ended. That is the age of the youngest rock below the gap. Two ages on one surface, because an unconformity is not an instant. It is a stretch of time where the record is missing, and the Wheeler chart later will need to know how long.

Scroll right to the tract column in the Tops table. Hold.

Now look at the column on the right, because you did not fill this one in. The table reads every consecutive pair of typed surfaces and works out which systems tract they bound.

Point at a lowstand row, then a transgressive one, then a highstand.

A subaerial unconformity or a correlative conformity below a maximum regressive surface bounds a lowstand. A maximum regressive surface below a maximum flooding surface bounds a transgressive tract. A maximum flooding surface below a basal surface of forced regression bounds a highstand. And that surface above a correlative conformity bounds the falling stage.

Find a row showing a question mark, and one naming no tract.

Where a pair spans two tracts, it names none. Where an internal boundary has not been picked yet, it shows a question mark. So this table is also a list of jobs. Every question mark is a surface you have not found yet.

05

Logging the rock

08:54 · 2:52 · 430 w
Screen
Voice over

Intervals view. Open the kind selector so all eight kinds are readable.

The Intervals view logs everything that happens between two depths rather than at one. Lithology. Core descriptions. Facies. Electrofacies. Depositional environment. Log motifs. Systems tracts. And biozones. One table, one kind at a time.

Choose lithology. Add two rows with a top, a base, a code and a grain size.

Start with lithology. Each row is a top depth, a base depth and a code from the vocabulary, with a grain size beside it.

Use Replace from paste. Paste a table using short codes. Hold as they resolve to full names.

Or paste the whole thing. Replace from paste takes a table of top, base and code, in feet or metres, and resolves the usual abbreviations. Sandstone, shale, limestone and dolomite all arrive from their short forms. A code it does not recognise is kept exactly as you typed it, rather than guessed at or dropped.

Create two overlapping lithology rows and show the refusal. Then switch kind to facies and create one overlapping a lithology interval, accepted.

Overlaps inside one kind are refused, because a metre of rock cannot be two lithologies. But a facies interval is allowed to overlap a lithology interval, because those are two different statements about the same rock.

Quick cuts: Well Data Manager's LAS import with its interval checkbox, then Petrophysics Studio's Facies publish button.

Three other doors fill this table without you typing anything. A L. A. S. three point zero file with a core or lithology block imports its intervals when you load the well. The crossplot facies you published from Petrophysics Studio arrive here as facies intervals. And the systems tracts we are about to record from the section land here too.

Core view. Upload a photograph with its top and base depth and a caption. Hold as the depth registered strip builds.

The Core view is the same panel you saw in Well Data Manager back in the first episode. Add a photograph with the depths it spans and a caption, and the strip on the left stacks your photographs in proportion to those spans.

Attempt an oversized image to show the refusal, then select an organisation-shared well showing the panel read-only.

Five megabytes per image and two hundred per well, refused before anything uploads rather than after. The photographs live in your private well storage, and a well somebody shared with you shows them read only.

Voice over

Section view. It opens on the section saved in Well Correlation. Hold.

Open the Section view, and there is your cross section. The same wells, the same order, the same template and the same datum you saved in Well Correlation, drawn by the same painter.

Zoom two or three tops drawn with different line styles, with their abbreviations in the tags.

The difference is that every top you typed now draws as its type. Different line styles for different surfaces, with the abbreviation in the tag. Build sections in Well Correlation. Interpret them here.

Turn on Tracts. Hold on the coloured fills, then find a hatched one.

Turn on Tracts and the intervals between your typed surfaces fill with colour by systems tract. Lowstand, transgressive, highstand, falling stage. And notice that some of them are hatched. A hatched fill means that tract needs a boundary you have not picked yet, so the app shows you what it thinks while telling you it is not sure.

Click Record tracts.

Those fills are implied until you press Record tracts. That writes them as real systems tract intervals on every well in the section that you own.

Pause 3 seconds. The tracts are written. Then cut to the Intervals view showing the new rows.

Intervals view, kind set to systems tract, showing the recorded rows. Then back to the section.

Go back to the Intervals view afterwards and they are there, editable, exactly like anything else you logged by hand.

Turn on Motifs. Show the outlined motif intervals beside the first track.

Motifs outlines your log motif intervals beside the first track, so a coarsening upward or a fining upward pattern reads at a glance.

Datum selector: choose Stretch between two surfaces and pick two of your typed surfaces. Hold on the rearranged section.

The datum selector is the one from Well Correlation, with the important option in it. Stretch between two surfaces takes the rock between two of your picks and stretches it onto two common lines, while everything above and below shifts rigidly. That is a chronostratigraphic assumption, and it is exactly the right one when your two surfaces are the same age everywhere.

Point at a well carrying only one of the two surfaces, and at the note saying it shifted onto that line.

A well that only carries one of the two surfaces shifts onto that single line, and says so, rather than pretending to a stretch it cannot make.

Set up a ghost curve between two wells and drag the shift. Then click Save view.

The ghost curve is here too, the same tool you used in the correlation episode. And Save view keeps your datum and your ghost with the stratigraphy project.

07

The Wheeler chart

14:49 · 2:39 · 390 w
Screen
Voice over

Click Wheeler.

And now the view that makes this whole application worth the trouble. Wheeler.

Pause 3 seconds. The chart draws. Hold wide so the whole thing is visible.

Trace the time axis with the cursor, top to bottom.

It re-plots that same section with geologic time running down the axis instead of depth. Every well becomes a column in time rather than in rock.

Point at a deposition cell spanning two ages, coloured by its tract. Then compare a thick interval and a thin one on the depth section.

The rock between two dated surfaces becomes a cell spanning the ages of those two surfaces, coloured by the tract they imply. Thick rock deposited quickly makes a short cell. Thin rock deposited slowly makes a tall one. The chart is about time, so thickness stops mattering.

Zoom the hatched hiatus cell. Cut briefly to the same unconformity on the depth section, where it is one line.

And this is the part a depth section can never show you. An unconformity with a hiatus end age becomes a hatched cell, and the height of that cell is how much time is missing. On a normal section an unconformity is a line. Here it is a gap you can measure, and it is often the tallest thing on the chart.

Point at the international stages drawn behind the columns.

The international stages sit behind the columns for reference.

Find a well listed as not placed and zoom the stated reason.

A well with fewer than two dated surfaces cannot be put on a time axis at all, so it is listed as not placed, with the reason. That list is your next job, not an error.

Deliberately date a deeper surface younger than a shallower one. Hold on the refusal naming the surface. Then correct it.

And if you have dated a deeper surface as younger than a shallower one, the chart refuses and names the surface. Ages between dated surfaces are interpolated at a constant rate, and a constant rate cannot run backwards.

Voice over

Ages view with the well selected. The age-depth plot draws.

The Ages view takes one well and plots age against depth.

Point along the segments joining dated surfaces, with the rate written on each. Contrast a steep one and a shallow one.

Your dated surfaces are joined by straight segments, each one a constant accumulation rate, and each rate is written on its segment. A steep segment is slow accumulation. A shallow one is fast.

Point at a hiatus bar, then at a stage label, then scroll to the table beneath.

A bar at each dated unconformity shows the hiatus, and the international stage of each surface is labelled. The table underneath repeats all of it in numbers.

Cut to the Intervals view showing biozone rows with a scheme and ages. Back to Ages, click Biozone datums. Show the new typed tops.

Two buttons turn this into work other people can use. If you logged biozones in the Intervals view, with a scheme and ages, Biozone datums turns every biozone range into two typed biozone tops carrying those ages and that scheme. Your palaeontology becomes dated surfaces, automatically.

Click Send to Basin. Show the created model with its layers.

And Send to Basin builds a model in Basin and Charge Modeling. One layer per top, ages taken from the bounding surfaces, the dominant lithology read from your lithology log, and every hiatus turned into an erosion event.

Point at an erosion amount waiting to be typed, and at an undated layer keeping placeholders. Then click Open Basin.

It will ask you how much rock each erosion event removed, because that is a judgment nobody can make for you. And an undated layer keeps placeholders and says so, rather than inventing an age. Open Basin takes you there, with this well remembered as the model's tie.

Turning description into a map

Voice over

Back in the Section view. Choose a tract, then click Map net sand.

One more handoff, and it is the one that turns all of this description into a map.

Mapping and Surface Studio opens with the source already set to the stratigraphy option.

Map net sand takes a tract's two bounding surfaces and opens Mapping and Surface Studio on the net sand between them, across every well in your section.

In Mapping, show the form: the measure options, the upper and lower top selectors, the lithology checkboxes.

In Mapping, that is the stratigraphy source we saw back in the fourth episode. Choose net sand, gross thickness or net to gross. Set the upper and the lower top. And tick which lithologies count as net, which is a decision you should make out loud rather than inherit from a default.

Point at the environment listed per well in the form, then click Grid.

Every well carrying both tops and a lithology log becomes a control point, and the form lists the environment that dominates the interval in each one. Then it grids, kriges, clips and publishes like any other surface.

After publishing, hover the surface row to show the provenance mentioning measured depth thickness.

One honest limitation, stated in the provenance. Those thicknesses are measured depth thicknesses between the two picks. On a deviated well that is not the true vertical thickness, and the surface says so rather than letting you assume otherwise.

Series position. This episode closes the loop Episode 1 opened. The Intervals and Core tabs were shown in Well Data Manager and nothing since has used them. Here they carry a whole workflow, and section 05 says so explicitly. If you are ordering the playlist for a new viewer, this one belongs after Well Correlation and not before it.

Audience note. This is the most specialist episode in the series and the only one aimed at a single discipline rather than at everybody. Pitch it that way in the description. A stratigrapher will watch all twenty three minutes; a reservoir engineer probably wants only sections 07 and 09.

Accuracy note. Every view, control name, surface type and refusal message in this script matches the shipping application at the time of writing. If a label changes, fix the script before the next take rather than talking around it on camera.
