Before you record

  1. Two published surfaces at minimum, ideally three. One from Mapping and one from Seismolord makes the point in section 02 that the model does not care which application produced them. Two is the engine minimum, because a model with one surface has no zone.
  2. The zones and tops must already exist. The ties in section 05 need well tops matching your surfaces, and the population in section 08 needs registry zones carrying per-well averages. Both come from the Petrophysics Studio work in Episode 2. Without them, half this episode has nothing to show.
  3. Engineer the kriging fallback. Section 08 is the best beat in the episode and it needs one fault block with four or more wells carrying a zone average, and another with fewer. Check your data produces both before the take, because the fallback report is the thing worth filming.
  4. Have a fault polygon and a boundary polygon ready. Both are drawn in Mapping and Surface Studio and both are listed here. Sections 02 and 07 use them.
  5. Render section by section, honour the pause. The single pause mark is the model building, which takes real time on a full stack.
The argument this episode is making. A good model reports its own compromises. Section 05 shows amber residuals where the model disagrees with a well. Section 08 shows kriging falling back to a plane or a mean, with the reason written down. Section 10 puts the population provenance directly under the volume table. Section 11 sends the viewer to the QC view before they show anybody anything. Protect those four.
Second theme, worth keeping. The model saves a recipe and not a grid, so every build recomputes from whatever the registry holds now. It is mentioned in section 04, paid off in section 10, and explained in section 11. It is what makes this model a living thing rather than a photograph.

Before the take

The fault splits the wells four west (Ekene-1, -3, -5, -7) to three east (Ekene-2, -4, -6). At the Ekene Sand only Ekene-6 and Ekene-10 carry a pay zone in the eastern block, because Ekene-2 and Ekene-4 found the sand wet. Everything you have made, in one object

Voice over

Dashboard, Geoscience module. Hold on the Earth Modeling tile, then open it.

Seven videos in, this Suite has quite a lot of your work sitting in it. Surfaces from mapping and from seismic. Tops from your correlation. Zone averages from your log analysis. Fault polygons you drew on a map. All of it correct, and all of it separate.

The workspace with a model already built, the map showing a property layer. Hold four seconds.

Earth Modeling is where those pieces become one object. It stacks your surfaces into a layer cake, ties that cake to the wells, splits it along your faults, fills every zone with porosity, water saturation and net to gross, and reports the rock volume of each zone in each block.

Switch to the QC & volumes view. Hold on the clamp report and the tie table.

And it does one more thing that matters more than any of that. It tells you where it had to make something up. Every surface it had to clamp. Every well that disagrees with it. Every block where there were not enough wells to interpolate properly. That report is the difference between a model and a picture of one.

Quick cuts, two seconds each: the model stack in the explorer, a section, the 3D framework, a volumes table.

In this video we will build a model from the surfaces the last three episodes produced, adjust it to the wells, populate it, and send its volumes onward. Let us stack some surfaces.

Stack the surfaces

Screen
Voice over

Explorer, Registry surfaces list. Point at one from Mapping, then one published from Seismolord.

The explorer on the left lists every surface in the registry. The ones you gridded in Mapping and Surface Studio, and the ones you published out of Seismolord, side by side, with no distinction between them. To this model they are the same kind of thing.

Click Add to stack on two or three surfaces. They appear under Model stack (shallow to deep).

Add two or more to the stack. Two is the minimum, because a model with one surface has no zone in it.

Use the up and down arrows to reorder the stack. Put one deliberately out of order, then fix it.

Then order them, shallowest at the top. The arrows do that. Get this right, because the whole model is built on the assumption that the stack is in order, and it will tell you loudly when it is not.

Builder dock, Model frame. Type a cell size, then choose the boundary polygon.

Over in the builder dock on the right, the frame. The model is built on the top surface's frame, at its cell size, unless you type a different one. And if you drew a boundary polygon in Mapping and Surface Studio, choose it here. Everything outside it will be empty on every surface, so your map, your section and your volumes all stop at the lease line rather than running off into somebody else's acreage.

03

Name the tops and the zones

03:20 · 1:50 · 275 w
Screen
Voice over

Builder dock, Tie tops (per stacked surface). Open the first dropdown.

Now the part that connects this model to everything you have already done. For each surface in the stack, say which well top it represents.

Hold on the dropdown showing the registry top names. Choose one per surface.

Those are the tops you picked in Well Correlation and in Petrophysics Studio. Naming them here is what lets the model compare itself against the wells, which it will do in a moment, and it is what makes every residual you are about to see mean something.

Zones (between consecutive surfaces). Choose a registry zone for each interval.

Underneath, the zones. Every interval between two consecutive surfaces is a zone, and each one maps to a registry zone name. That is where the porosity, the water saturation and the net to gross come from. The zone averages you computed per well back in the second episode are the control points this model will interpolate between.

Leave one surface on no tie and one zone unset. Point at both.

You do not have to fill in everything. A surface with no tie is simply not adjusted, and a zone with no registry name gets no properties. But every one you can fill in makes the model better constrained, and it costs you one dropdown.

04

Build, and read what it did

05:10 · 2:15 · 330 w
Screen
Voice over

Ribbon, click Build model.

Then Build model.

Pause 3 seconds. The build runs and the map draws.

Zoom the status bar so the whole build report is legible.

It resamples every surface onto one frame, clamps them into order, ties them to your wells, populates every zone and sums the volumes, then tells you what it did in the status bar. Read that line.

Map layer selector. Step through zone top, zone base, thickness, porosity, Sw, NTG, fault blocks, holding on each.

The map now shows any layer of any zone. The top and the base as depth. The thickness between them. The porosity, the water saturation and the net to gross you have just populated. And the fault blocks themselves.

Ribbon, toggle the depth unit from feet to metres and back. Let the map labels relabel.

The depth unit button is the same setting you have been using across Geoscience, so the model speaks in whatever unit you have been reading maps in. Underneath, it computes in metres, positive down from the datum, and it says so.

Ribbon, change the volume units from metric to field.

Volume units are a separate choice, because the units you want for a rock volume are rarely the units you want for a barrel. Metric gives you millions of cubic metres throughout. Field gives you acre feet for rock and net, and million barrels for pore and hydrocarbon pore volume.

Change the cell size slightly and press Build model again. Let it recompute.

And every build recomputes everything from scratch. Nothing is cached, and nothing is stale. That sounds like a performance decision and it is really an honesty decision. We will come back to why at the end.

05

Ties, residuals and adjustment

07:25 · 2:40 · 400 w
Screen
Voice over

QC & volumes view. Scroll to the tie table and hold.

Open QC and volumes, and find the tie table. This is the model's confession.

Point along one row: the well, the surface, the residual.

For every well and every tied surface, it takes the top's true vertical depth below datum, worked out through that well's deviation survey and its kelly bushing, and compares it against where the surface actually sits at that position. The difference is the residual. Positive means the pick is deeper than the surface.

Find and zoom an amber residual.

Anything over ten metres turns amber. An amber residual is not automatically an error. It might be the surface, it might be the pick, or it might be a well nobody used when the surface was gridded. But it is always worth a look, and this table is where you find out before anybody else does.

Builder dock, Well adjustment. Turn it on and click Build model.

Then, if you want the model to honour your picks exactly, turn on well adjustment and build again.

QC panel, the adjustment card showing maximum residual before and after per surface, then the tie table's Before column.

Each tied surface is now warped through its own residuals before the clamping happens. It is exact at the well, half the correction at half the radius, and nothing at all beyond the radius. Look at the adjustment card. It gives you the maximum residual before and after, per surface, and the tie table gains a Before column. You can see exactly how much you moved.

Type a small radius, build, hold on the map. Then a large one, build, hold again.

The radius defaults to three times the median spacing between your ties, which is a reasonable guess. Type your own when you know better. A large radius pulls the whole surface toward one well. A small one leaves a dimple around it. Neither is wrong. Both are a decision, and this is where you make it.

Optional break: end of Part 1 The model is built and tied to the wells, which is a complete result to stop on. Part 1 runs 10:05. Part 2 opens on section 06 with a ten second recap of the built stack.
06

Derived horizons

10:05 · 1:38 · 245 w
Screen
Voice over

Builder dock, Derived horizons. Open the kind selector showing both options.

Sometimes the surface you need was never mapped. Derived horizons make one from the surfaces you already have.

Choose the parallel kind. Pick a source surface, type a thickness. Then switch the thickness for an isochore surface from the registry.

Parallel takes a source surface and offsets it by a thickness you type. A negative thickness places it above. Or, instead of a constant, point it at an isochore surface in the registry, and the offset varies across the field the way a real thickness does.

Choose the proportional kind. Pick a source and a base surface, set the fraction to 0.5.

Proportional puts the new horizon a fraction of the way between two surfaces. A half is midway. That is how you split a thick zone into an upper and a lower unit that follow the shape of both bounding surfaces, rather than the shape of just one of them.

The derived horizon appears in the model stack. Order it, tie it to a top, then remove it and add it back.

Once it exists it joins the stack like any other surface. Order it, tie it to a top, build zones on it, publish layers from it. Remove it from the dock and it leaves the stack again.

Screen

Explorer, Fault polygons from Mapping. Add one to the model.

A layer cake with no faults in it is a layer cake that does not exist. The explorer lists the fault polygons you drew in Mapping and Surface Studio, and adding one splits the model.

Builder dock, fault drawing. Draw a polygon straight on the map, name it, save it.

08

Filling the zones

13:21 · 2:44 · 410 w
Screen
Voice over

Builder dock, Population method. Open the selector showing all four.

Now fill the zones. The population method decides how the model gets from a handful of well averages to a value in every cell, and there are four ways to do it.

Choose the constant method, build, and hold on the flat property map.

Constant gives every cell in a block the interval weighted mean of the wells in that block. It is flat, it is defensible, and when you have two wells it is honestly all you know.

Choose the trend method, build, and hold on the sloped map.

Trend fits a least squares plane through the wells of the block. Use it when the property genuinely has a regional gradient and you have enough wells to see it.

Choose ordinary kriging. The variogram panel appears. Click the fit button, then build.

Ordinary kriging is the one you want when the data supports it. It fits a variogram from the control points of each block, removes the trend first, and interpolates properly. The variogram panel is right beside it, with a fit button that reads the range and the sill off your own points.

Map layer, choose Porosity kriging variance. Point at a low area near wells, then a high area away from them.

And like the kriging in Mapping and Surface Studio, it gives you a variance map. Switch the map layer to the porosity kriging variance. Low near the wells, high where the value is a guess. You can publish that variance as an attribute, which is a far better answer to how confident are you than any single number.

QC view. Find the block that fell back and zoom the line stating the reason.

But here is the beat that matters most in this whole application. Ordinary kriging needs four wells in a block. If a block has fewer, the model does not fail, and it does not silently pretend. It falls back to a plane. If it cannot do that, it falls back to the mean. And every single fallback is written into QC with the reason it happened.

Cut between the beautiful kriged map and the fallback line in QC.

So when you are looking at a beautiful kriged porosity map, go and check that report. Some of those blocks may be a single number wearing a colour ramp, and you would rather know.

10

Volumes, publish, save

18:05 · 2:48 · 420 w
Screen
Voice over

QC & volumes, scroll to a zone's volume table. Hold.

Then the numbers. Every zone gets a volume table, per block and in total.

Point along the four columns in turn.

Bulk volume is thickness times cell area. Net multiplies by net to gross. Pore multiplies by porosity. And hydrocarbon pore volume multiplies by one minus the water saturation. Four columns, each one a single multiplication away from the last, so you can see exactly where the volume goes.

Point at the population provenance line under the table.

Under each table sits the provenance. Which method populated that zone, in which block, and where it fell back. The number and the reason to believe it, in the same place.

Hover the Open in ReservoirCalc Pro button on the ribbon.

One boundary worth knowing. This is rock volume. There are no fluid contacts here, no recovery factor and no uncertainty. Those live in ReservoirCalc Pro, and the button on the ribbon takes you there.

With a layer shown on the map, click Publish layer. Then cut to Mapping and Surface Studio showing it in the list.

Publish layer sends whatever the map is showing back into the registry. A zone top or base goes as a structure surface. A thickness goes as an isochore. A property or a variance goes as an attribute. All of them carry the model name, the zone and the methods in their provenance, so the surface knows where it came from.

Click Volumes CSV. Open the downloaded file and hold on its header lines.

Volumes C. S. V. downloads every zone's table in your chosen units, with the frame and the provenance written into the header of the file itself.

Builder dock, Saved models. Type a model name and save. Then load it back and let it rebuild.

And Save model definition keeps the recipe. Not the grids. The recipe. Which surfaces, in what order, tied to which tops, with which zones, which population method and which radius. Load it later and it rebuilds every grid from scratch.

Wrap up

20:53 · 1:26 · 215 w
Screen
Voice over

Pull back to the built model, the map beside the 3D framework.

So that is Earth Modeling. Surfaces from two applications, tops from a third, zone averages from a fourth, faults from a fifth, assembled into one model that reports its own volumes.

Hold on the Saved models section of the dock.

And that recipe is the reason every build recomputes everything. A saved grid is a photograph of what you believed on a Tuesday. A saved recipe rebuilds itself from whatever the registry holds now. Re-pick a top, re-grid a surface, add a well, press Build, and the model updates instead of going stale in a folder.

Switch to the QC view one last time. Pan across the clamp report, an amber residual, and a fallback line.

One habit to leave you with. When a model looks good, go straight to the QC view before you show it to anybody. Read the clamped nodes. Read the amber residuals. Read the fallbacks. A model with nothing to confess is rare. One that hides what it has is worse.

Series position. This is the widest episode in the series. Section 01 names five earlier applications by their output, and the model is assembled from all of them. If you are cutting a trailer for the whole playlist, the build in section 04 is the shot to use, because it is the only moment where everything the viewer has watched arrives on screen at once.
